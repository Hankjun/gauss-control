Heat Treatment and Controlled Atmosphere Fundamentals

Common Heat Treatment Atmospheres: Endogas, Nitrogen, Methanol, Dissociated Ammonia, and Vacuum

Quick answer

Commonly used atmospheres for heat treatment can be divided into three categories: generating atmosphere (endothermic RX gas, exothermic gas, dissociated ammonia), synthetic atmosphere (nitrogen-methanol, nitrogen-hydrogen, pure nitrogen) and vacuum/low pressure. Endothermic gas (about 20% CO, 40% H₂, 40% N₂) has stable composition and is the mainstream carrier gas for carburizing and neutral quenching; nitrogen-methanol is easy to implement but has large composition fluctuations; dissociated ammonia (75% H₂, 25% N₂) is mostly used for bright annealing and nitriding dilution.

  • Endothermic RX gas: about 20% CO + 40% H₂ + 40% N₂ (natural gas raw material), suitable for carburizing and heating before quenching
  • Nitrogen-methanol: simulates endothermic composition, easy to implement but affected by temperature and drip stability
  • dissociated ammonia: 75% H₂ + 25% N₂, used for bright annealing and nitriding dilution
  • Selection depends on process, output, safety and hazardous chemicals management costs

Why are endothermic atmospheres most commonly used?

The endothermic gas is produced by "directional cracking" in the generator and is stable in composition, especially providing a relatively constant CO ratio, which is critical for carbon potential calculations (carbon potential calculations require known CO%). Cracking is an endothermic reaction, and the heat is provided by the generator. The gas will not disturb the furnace temperature after entering the heat treatment furnace.

Characteristics of nitrogen-methanol

The nitrogen-methanol system has low investment and fast start-up. The common ratio is about 1.1 m³ nitrogen corresponding to 1 L of methanol, which results in a composition similar to the endothermic type. However, the degree of methanol cracking is affected by the furnace temperature and furnace type, and the CO content will fluctuate. The on-site monitoring case of Gosi Control shows that the CO of a certain nitrogen-methanol integral-quench furnace changes between about 19% and 22% within a month, which can bring about a carbon potential deviation of about ±0.12.

Selection ideas

  1. Process requirements: Carburizing and quenching require controllable carbon potential → endothermic or nitrogen-methanol; bright annealing → ammonia decomposition, nitrogen-hydrogen or exothermic;
  2. Production volume and number of furnaces: Multiple furnaces, continuous production → endothermic gas generators with centralized air supply are more economical;
  3. Security and Compliance: Methanol and liquid ammonia are hazardous chemicals, and the storage and management costs cannot be ignored;
  4. Quality system: Automotive industry CQI-9 has strict requirements on carbon potential accuracy, and atmosphere stability is a prerequisite.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between propane and natural gas for endothermic gas?

Propane feedstock produces gas with higher CO (approximately 23%) and lower H₂ (approximately 31%), with slightly different carbon transfer efficiencies than natural gas feedstock. The choice usually depends on local gas supply.

Will vacuum low pressure carburizing replace gas carburizing?

Low-pressure carburizing has grown significantly in the high-end field, but equipment investment and operating costs are high. For large quantities of automobile parts, controlled atmosphere carburization is still the mainstream, and the two will coexist for a long time.

Which is cheaper, nitrogen shielding or endothermic atmosphere?

Depends on local nitrogen and gas prices, gas usage and number of furnaces. In large-volume, multi-furnace applications, centralized gas supply from endothermic gas generators usually has lower operating costs; in small-volume applications, nitrogen-based atmosphere is more flexible.
